Abstract
Introduction
The principal gap that this report aims to bridge lies at the intersection of **soil–structure interaction modeling** and **machine learning-compatible simulation design** for **bio-inspired burrowing systems**.

While discrete element methods (DEM) have traditionally dominated soil–probe interaction studies, their computational intensity severely limits their applicability for large-scale, dynamic, or learning-based simulations (Pytlos et al., 2015). DEM codes typically prioritize micromechanical accuracy at the cost of simulation speed, making them unsuitable for applications where thousands of episodes are required to train reinforcement learning (RL) agents. Conversely, real-time capable physics engines like Box2D offer rapid and stable integration schemes but have not been extensively validated for geomechanical problems, particularly stress–dilatancy phenomena fundamental to granular behavior. It has already been demonstrated by Pytos et al that rigid-body physics engines, with proper calibration, can replicate fundamental soil mechanical behavior** such as Rowe’s stress–dilatancy response, thus validating their use beyond entertainment applications and into scientific geomechanics.
Moreover, existing bio-inspired burrowing models (e.g., Zhang et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2021) often focus either on physical realism—requiring complex probe morphologies and full cycle soil–fluid interactions—or on static simplifications that omit the dynamic coupling needed for learning tasks. Consequently, there is a lack of simulation frameworks that are **both physically grounded and computationally efficient**, capable of **capturing soil deformation by probe-burrowing** while **supporting sequential decision-making and control policy optimization**.

This report therefore proposes to bridge the key technical gap:
**Structuring the simulation environment explicitly for RL compatibility** by simplifying the probe’s burrowing mechanism into discrete, observable actions and state transitions within a Markov Decision Process (MDP) framework.
By addressing these gaps, the work advances toward the practical development of **autonomous, adaptive burrowing probes** for geotechnical exploration, planetary science missions, and biomimetic robotics, where both physical fidelity and scalable learning are critical requirements.

Aims and Objectives
The aim of this study is to develop and validate a computational framework for simulating bio-inspired burrowing probes using a rigid-body physics engine (Box2D), thereby enabling the design of autonomous site investigation tools that can operate efficiently in granular soils. Addressing a key gap between biological burrowing strategies and their numerical replication, the project seeks to demonstrate that simplified dynamic simulations can accurately capture essential soil-probe interactions, while remaining compatible with reinforcement learning (RL) methodologies for future autonomy development.
The specific objectives of the study are to:
	Construct a physically consistent soil model within Box2D by implementing Newton–Euler rigid-body dynamics, constraint-based contact resolution, and calibrated timestep and friction schemes.
	Validate the soil model’s macromechanical behavior through biaxial compression tests of 32 hexagonally packed discs, using ray-cast identified boundaries and a moving top platen, with stress–dilatancy behavior benchmarked against Rowe’s theoretical curve.
	Design and implement a simplified anchor–tip burrowing sequence suitable for RL applications, consisting of discrete shaft expansion, tip extension, contraction, and downward advancement phases.
	Define the probe’s action space and state transitions in the format of a Markov Decision Process (MDP), establishing a foundation for subsequent machine learning training.
Each objective is intended to build a verifiable link between physical modeling fidelity, behavioral validation, and simulation readiness for intelligent control development.

2. Literature Review
2.1 Introduction
The challenge of designing a self-burrowing anchor system intersects multiple domains, including soil mechanics, computational modeling, and machine learning. This literature review critically examines four key thematic areas relevant to the project aims: (1) static-controlled versus dynamic burrowing models, (2) computational efficiency and scalability of soil simulation methods, (3) validation of soil behavior (with emphasis on stress–dilatancy), and (4) compatibility of the burrowing problem with reinforcement learning (RL) and Markov Decision Process (MDP) formalism. Each theme is analyzed in the context of constructing a physically consistent soil model in Box2D, validating its macromechanical behavior, and leveraging it for an RL-driven burrowing strategy. The review highlights current findings, limitations, and gaps, linking each theme to the project’s specific aims. The section concludes with a synthesis of insights and identification of research gaps that motivate the present work.
2.2 Theme 1: Static-Controlled versus Dynamic Burrowing Models
Traditional penetration into granular soil is often approached as a static or quasi-static process – for example, pushing a cone or anchor at a slow rate to simulate steady penetration resistance (as in a cone penetration test, CPT). In such static-controlled models, resistance arises from continuous soil densification and friction mobilization ahead of the penetrator. In contrast, many biological burrowers (e.g. razor clams, worms) employ dynamic cyclic motions (expanding, contracting, and moving) to reduce effective soil resistance and energy expenditure. Huang and Tao (2020) notably simplified the Atlantic razor clam’s burrowing kinematics into four major sequential steps – a cylindrical cavity expansion, a cone penetration, followed by contraction and retraction – analogous to geotechnical pressuremeter and cone penetration testsfile-lnswhv6gosfflhnmdnxkp8. This cyclic strategy essentially “tricks” the soil by first expanding to create a local cavity (weakening the soil’s structure or reducing confinement), then advancing the tip with less resistance, and repeating. By comparing this biomimetic dynamic penetration to a purely static push, Huang and Tao’s study provides critical insight: the dynamic, clam-inspired sequence dramatically lowers the penetration forces and energy required for the cone advancement. Specifically, their analytical and discrete element method (DEM) models showed that cyclic expansion of the shaft causes a stress release around the cone, leading to a reduction in tip resistance during subsequent penetrationfile-lnswhv6gosfflhnmdnxkp8. As a result, the energetic cost for driving the cone and shaft was reduced by about 36% in the dynamic approach compared to a static continuous penetration (cone-only) strategyfile-lnswhv6gosfflhnmdnxkp8. This indicates that a properly timed sequence of expansion and penetration can exploit soil mechanics to achieve penetration more efficiently than any monotonic push.
Later studies have reinforced and expanded these findings. Chen et al. (2021) simulated a similar “anchor–tip” burrowing strategy using a calibrated 3D DEM, breaking the process into three stages: (1) an initial pseudo-static cone penetration (CP), (2) an anchor expansion (AE) to develop anchorage, and (3) a self-propelled advancement stage (SP) where the tip is pushed deeper while the anchor holds, using controlled motionsfile-yebkrdecjtehwpefrowkzb. Their results confirm that radial anchor expansion temporarily reduces the soil’s penetration resistance, especially when the expandable anchor is positioned closer to the tip and has larger expansion magnitudefile-yebkrdecjtehwpefrowkzb. This dynamic resistance reduction is precisely the interference effect noted by Huang and Tao – by altering the stress field (via expansion) before advancing the tip, the probe faces less opposition from the soil. Such an effect cannot be captured in static models that lack intermediate motions. Chen et al. further demonstrated that key design parameters (anchor length, expansion amount, anchor-tip distance, and surface friction) strongly influence burrowing success: longer anchors with greater expansion and higher friction mobilize larger anchorage forces, enabling the tip to overcome soil resistance more effectivelyfile-yebkrdecjtehwpefrowkzbfile-yebkrdecjtehwpefrowkzb. Another notable finding is the role of depth (overburden pressure) – increasing the depth (and thus confining stress) improved the self-penetration ability of the probe by providing greater normal stress for the anchor to push against, up to a pointfile-yebkrdecjtehwpefrowkzb. This implies dynamic burrowing strategies might actually become more effective in deeper or more consolidated soils (where static penetration would be extremely difficult), an observation echoed by analogies to earthworm peristaltic burrowing in dense soilsfile-yebkrdecjtehwpefrowkzb. The trade-off, however, is that in denser or stronger soils the probe must exert more force during expansion; the dynamic strategy is only advantageous if it can generate sufficient anchorage to exceed the increased penetration resistance. Chen et al. conclude that the anchor–tip mechanism could enable burrowing to tens of meters depth with much lighter, portable equipment than conventional drills, given its ability to generate large anchorage forces in situfile-yebkrdecjtehwpefrowkzb. This highlights the potential of dynamic burrowing for applications in low-accessibility sites (e.g. near infrastructure or in planetary exploration), directly motivating the present project’s Aim 3 of designing a simplified dynamic anchor-tip burrowing sequence for an autonomous agent.
A critical perspective is gained by comparing energy efficiency in static vs dynamic approaches. Whereas Huang and Tao (2020) observed a net energy saving with their clam-inspired cycle, Zhang et al. (2023) found a more complex outcome in a dual-anchor self-burrowing probe model. In their DEM simulations, a tip oscillation (a small back-and-forth lateral movement of the tip) was introduced during the penetration phase to further reduce resistancefile-5ef7w8j8s7pr9mhztd3vtp. This indeed enabled continuous burrowing without external push, but at a cost: the oscillation mechanism consumed over 50% of the total work in each burrowing cyclefile-5ef7w8j8s7pr9mhztd3vtp. In fact, the total mechanical work required for one cycle of burrowing was greater than that of an equivalent depth static penetration at constant ratefile-5ef7w8j8s7pr9mhztd3vtp. In other words, the dynamic strategy demanded more energy overall, even though it achieved penetration incrementally. This apparent contradiction is resolved when considering peak force versus total energy: the dynamic cycle significantly reduced the peak force or instantaneous power needed (staying within what a small robot can supply), at the expense of doing additional motions (oscillations) that accumulate more work. Chen et al. (2023) (who studied multi-cycle dual-anchor burrowing in varying soil densities) similarly report that most of the work in each cycle is done by the tip oscillation, especially in dense soil, and that while oscillation “aids in enabling self-burrowing to greater depths, it also produces a greater work demand”par.nsf.govpar.nsf.gov. These findings critically indicate that dynamic burrowing is not inherently more energy-efficient in terms of absolute work — rather, its benefit lies in manageably partitioning the work and exploiting moments of lowered resistance. For practical design, this means the control strategy must be optimized: excessive or inefficient motions could squander energy, but well-timed actions can minimize peak loads and avoid soil failure modes that would otherwise halt progress.
In summary, literature on burrowing models underscores that a static-controlled approach (continuous push) is fundamentally limited by high resistance and may require impractically large forces for deep penetration, whereas dynamic-controlled or cyclic strategies can dramatically improve penetrability through stress manipulation (e.g. cavity expansion and tip oscillation). The dynamic approach, however, introduces a balance between force reduction and energy expenditure. These insights directly inform Project Aim 3: the burrowing sequence for the RL agent should leverage dynamic actions (e.g. anchor expansion, staged tip advancement, possibly oscillation) to surmount soil resistance with limited actuator force, while also being mindful of efficiency. The literature provides a basis for which actions are effective (and why), guiding the design of action primitives and the target behavior for our simulated anchor system.
2.3 Theme 2: Computational Efficiency and Scalability
Realizing a physically detailed soil interaction model for burrowing poses significant computational challenges. The Discrete Element Method (DEM) has been the standard approach for simulating granular media in geomechanics since the pioneering work of Cundall & Strack (1979). DEM explicitly models individual particles and contacts, and can reproduce complex soil behaviors, but it is notoriously computationally intensive. Simulation run times grow quickly with the number of particles, contact interactions, and time steps required, making DEM often too slow for iterative design or learning applications. As Pytlos et al. (2015) note, DEM has been “handicapped by long simulation run times”, and despite advances in computing power, general single-processor speed improvements have stagnatedeprints.whiterose.ac.uk. Even with high-performance parallel computing (e.g. GPU or supercomputers), DEM can usually only be accelerated for those with access to such resourceseprints.whiterose.ac.uk. This is problematic for our project’s aims: training a reinforcement learning agent (Aim 4) requires running hundreds or thousands of simulation episodes, which would be infeasible if each episode (burrowing sequence) took hours or days to simulate with a high-fidelity DEM. Therefore, a key focus in the literature has been exploring alternative simulation methods that balance physical realism with far greater efficiency.
One promising approach is the use of physics engines originally developed for games and computer graphics, such as Box2D and Bullet. Pytlos, Gilbert and Smith (2015) demonstrated that a generic rigid-body physics engine (Box2D) can be repurposed to simulate granular soil behavior with surprising fidelityeprints.whiterose.ac.uk. A physics engine treats particles as rigid bodies and resolves contacts through constraint solvers or penalty forces in real-time, sacrificing some micro-scale accuracy for speed. Pytlos et al. showed that with appropriate modeling choices (e.g. representing sand grains as convex polygons rather than simple discs to better resist unrealistic rolling), Box2D could “faithfully model” particulate assemblies and even capture critical-state soil response (discussed further in Theme 3)eprints.whiterose.ac.uk. They conclude that Box2D is a viable alternative to custom DEM codes for two-dimensional simulations, providing a “simple and intuitive physics model” that can reproduce essential macro-scale behaviorseprints.whiterose.ac.uk. The implications are significant: physics engines are highly optimized for speed, often executing in real-time or faster, and can handle thousands of bodies on ordinary hardware. In Pytlos et al.’s simulations, on the order of 5,000 polygonal particles were usedeprints.whiterose.ac.uk, a scale that would be very slow in DEM but is tractable in Box2D. Similarly, Izadi and Bezuijen (2015) found that the 3D Bullet physics engine could simulate granular soil in a realistic mannerfile-uxersvwefdxbpegkblnzdy, reinforcing the trend that off-the-shelf engines can serve as fast computational sandboxes for soil. The trade-off is that these engines use approximate constraint-solving (e.g. iterative solvers for contacts, often allowing slight overlaps with corrective impulseseprints.whiterose.ac.ukeprints.whiterose.ac.uk) rather than solving contact forces with high precision as DEM does. Thus, careful parameter tuning and calibration are needed to ensure stability and realism (e.g. selecting time steps, solver iterations, and contact friction coefficients to match physical behavior)eprints.whiterose.ac.ukeprints.whiterose.ac.uk. Nonetheless, for many macro-scale outcomes of interest (stress–strain response, failure patterns), the literature suggests physics engines can produce acceptable accuracy with orders-of-magnitude faster computation.
Adopting a fast simulation method is crucial for Aim 1 of the project: constructing a physically consistent soil model in Box2D. The choice of Box2D is substantiated by the above studies. It offers the ability to run large numbers of burrowing trials for RL training (Aim 4) without the computational bottleneck of DEM. Moreover, since Box2D is open-source and relatively easy to integrate with machine learning frameworks, it facilitates the iterative experimentation needed in designing the burrowing strategy (Aim 3). However, achieving scalability must not compromise physical consistency. A critical analysis from the literature is that 2D simulations inherently simplify the problem – for instance, soil in a 2D (planar) simulation cannot rearrange out-of-plane, and the absolute values of friction angle or dilation might differ from true 3D behavior. Pytlos et al. (2015) acknowledge such limitations but demonstrate qualitatively correct trends, which is a reasonable trade-off for early-stage development and RL prototyping. Another consideration is how large of a domain and how many particles are needed to simulate the burrowing process without boundary effects. A balance must be struck between model detail and computational load. The reviewed studies provide guidance: using polygonal particles to prevent excessive rolling (hence capturing realistic shear resistance)eprints.whiterose.ac.ukeprints.whiterose.ac.uk, controlling time-step and solver iterations to maintain quasi-static conditions despite using a dynamic engineeprints.whiterose.ac.ukeprints.whiterose.ac.uk, and devising boundary conditions (e.g. periodic or pressure-controlled boundaries) to mimic an infinite soil masseprints.whiterose.ac.uk. By leveraging these insights, the project can build a Box2D simulation that runs efficiently yet remains grounded in known soil mechanics. This directly supports Aim 1, and is instrumental for Aim 3 and 4 by enabling rapid prototyping and learning. In summary, the literature advocates that computational efficiency in granular simulation is attainable via physics engines, which is a necessary enabler for the RL approach to burrowing that would otherwise be prohibitively slow with conventional methods.
2.4 Theme 3: Validation of Soil Behavior — Stress–Dilatancy
Ensuring the physical realism of the soil model is paramount; an efficient simulation is of little use if it fails to replicate fundamental soil behaviors. A key aspect in granular soil mechanics is the relationship between shear deformation, stress state, and volumetric change – commonly characterized by stress–dilatancy theory. Rowe’s (1962) stress–dilatancy relation provides a theoretical benchmark, linking the mobilized frictional resistance of a particle assembly to its dilatancy (volume contraction or expansion) during shear. In essence, densely packed soils tend to dilate (increase in volume) when sheared, resulting in a peak in shear strength, whereas loose soils contract. Eventually, both converge to a critical state where no further volume change occurs and shear stress reaches a steady value. Validating that the simulation reproduces this characteristic behavior is a direct goal of Aim 2: validating macromechanical behavior via biaxial compression tests and Rowe’s stress–dilatancy curve.
The literature shows encouraging evidence that even simplified models (like our Box2D approach) can capture these macromechanical phenomena. Pytlos et al. (2015) conducted biaxial compression tests in their Box2D granular model for both loose and dense specimens to check for realistic stress–strain and volume change behavior. The results were indeed consistent with laboratory observations and critical state soil mechanics theory. For a dense sample, the simulation exhibited a peak in mobilized friction angle (shear strength) at small strains, followed by a drop toward a lower steady value as the sample dilated and eventually reached critical stateeprints.whiterose.ac.uk. The loose sample, in contrast, showed no pronounced peak but a gradual increase in mobilized friction as it compacted (contracted) initially, also approaching the same steady-state strength at large strainseprints.whiterose.ac.uk. Notably, in both cases the mobilized friction angle converged to approximately 21.5°, which was the imposed critical-state friction angle for the particleseprints.whiterose.ac.uk. This indicates the model accurately reproduces the concept of a unique critical state strength regardless of initial density, a hallmark of real granular soils. The volumetric responses were likewise telling: dense samples dilated (increased in volume, i.e. negative volumetric strain) as shear progressed, while loose samples contracted, and both tended toward a similar final void ratio at critical stateeprints.whiterose.ac.ukeprints.whiterose.ac.uk. Figure 4 in Pytlos et al. shows these trends clearly, mirroring the stress–dilatancy behavior observed in physical experiments (e.g. triaxial or biaxial tests on sand)eprints.whiterose.ac.uk. In addition to macroscopic tests, Pytlos et al. also explicitly tested a simple direct shear scenario derived from Rowe’s theory: a ring of 32 particles sheared under confinement. The outcome matched Rowe’s predicted stress ratio for the given particle friction and dilation geometryeprints.whiterose.ac.uk, further confirming that the simulation honors classical soil mechanics relations.
These findings are significant because they validate that a Box2D-based model, despite its simplifications, can achieve quantitative agreement with known soil behavior when properly calibrated. The ability to capture dilatancy means the model responds to loading in a physically consistent way – if the probe (anchor) causes the soil to shear and dilate, the resistance will mobilize a peak and then drop, etc., as it should. For our project, similar validation exercises will be performed (e.g. simulated biaxial compression tests on the particulate domain) to ensure that the chosen parameters yield realistic stress–strain curves and friction angles. The literature (e.g. Pytlos et al.) provides a methodology for such validation: prepare samples at different initial densities, apply confining pressure and shear (via moving boundaries), and observe if the Rowe stress–dilatancy relationship emerges in the results. Success in this validation underpins Aim 2 and is tightly linked to Aim 1 – a “physically consistent” model by definition must replicate these well-established behaviors. If discrepancies arise (for instance, if the model shows excessive particle interlock or unrealistic rolling leading to abnormal peaks), the remedy may involve adjusting the particle shape distribution, contact friction coefficient, or damping parameters as suggested by prior studieseprints.whiterose.ac.ukeprints.whiterose.ac.uk. Another lesson from the literature is the importance of calibrating micro-scale properties (like particle friction μ) to match macro-scale outcomes (like peak friction angle φ). For example, Pytlos et al. used a particle friction μ of 0.6 to achieve a macro friction angle ~21°, which is somewhat low for real sands but reasonable in 2D where the absence of out-of-plane resistance yields lower apparent frictioneprints.whiterose.ac.uk. This reminds us that direct numerical values may not correspond one-to-one with real 3D soil, but the trend and form of the behavior can be aligned with theory.
In conclusion, the literature supports that our simplified soil model can be rigorously validated against soil mechanics principles, particularly Rowe’s stress–dilatancy theory. By reproducing critical state behavior and correct dilation/contraction trends, we ensure that the model not only runs fast (Theme 2) but also remains physically credible. This validation is crucial before tackling the controlled burrowing problem; otherwise, the RL agent might exploit unphysical artifacts of the simulation. Addressing Theme 3 thus directly contributes to achieving Aim 2, and it provides confidence that Aims 3 and 4 will be built on a sound representation of reality.
2.5 Theme 4: Compatibility with Reinforcement Learning and MDP Formalism
The final theme bridges the domain of geotechnical modeling with that of autonomous decision-making: how can the burrowing task be formulated as a Reinforcement Learning problem, and how compatible is the simulated environment with the Markov Decision Process (MDP) formalism? In an RL framework, the agent (here, the burrowing probe/anchor controller) interacts with an environment (the soil simulation in Box2D) by observing a state, taking actions, and receiving rewards. The project aims to define suitable state and action spaces for RL training (Aim 4) and ensure the environment provides the necessary feedback for learning. Literature on robotics and physics-based RL provides guidance and analogous examples for this setup.
A classic example is the OpenAI Gym LunarLander-v2 environment, which involves controlling a spacecraft in a 2D physics simulation (using the Box2D engine) to land softly on a pad. This problem, while different in context, is structurally similar: it has continuous state variables describing the vehicle’s position, velocity, orientation, etc., and a discrete set of actions (fire left thruster, right thruster, main engine, or do nothing). Importantly, it has been successfully solved with RL algorithms. Gadgil et al. (2020) applied both Sarsa and Deep Q-Network (DQN) algorithms to the LunarLander environment, achieving stable control policies. The environment is fully defined by a continuous state vector and a handful of discrete actions, and is Markovian (the next state depends only on the current state and action)file-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyi. Their best DQN agent attained high cumulative rewards (averaging over 200 per episode) in the standard settingfile-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyi, and even under added dynamics uncertainties (random disturbances and noise), the learned policy remained robust, still achieving positive rewards above 100 on averagefile-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyi. This demonstrates that an RL agent can learn to handle a physics-driven simulation with significant uncertainty and complexity. One key takeaway was the benefit of using a deep neural network function approximator (as in DQN) to handle the continuous state space. The Sarsa agent in their study required discretizing the state into bins, which inevitably loses information, whereas the DQN agent took the raw continuous state and output action Q-values directlyfile-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyi. Consequently, DQN outperformed Sarsa, highlighting that preserving state fidelity is crucial for optimal learningfile-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyi. Applying this insight to our context: the state for the burrowing task should be represented in a continuous or high-resolution manner (e.g. using actual real-valued features like tip depth, velocities, soil reaction forces) rather than coarsely discretized, to allow the neural network-based agent to fully exploit subtle cues in the environment. Box2D, being a continuous physics engine, naturally provides continuous states (positions, forces, etc.), so the challenge is selecting which state variables are most relevant and sufficient for decision-making (to satisfy the Markov property).
Designing the action space requires balancing simplicity and effectiveness. From Theme 1, the dynamic burrowing sequence can be broken down into a set of discrete actions or maneuvers: for example, an action to expand or contract the anchor, an action to advance the tip forward (or retract it backward), and possibly an action to oscillate or wiggle the tip. Each of these could be treated as a distinct action in an MDP formulation. By stringing together these actions in various orders and durations, the agent can theoretically recreate any dynamic burrowing cycle. The literature suggests that even complex cyclic strategies can be learned as sequences of primitive actions if the reward is appropriately shaped. In the Lunar Lander analogy, the agent learned to fire thrusters in short bursts to control descent; similarly, our agent might learn to alternate between expanding the anchor and pushing the tip at the right cadence to maximize depth. We anticipate defining a reward function that incentivizes burrowing deeper while minimizing energy or avoiding failure (e.g. a positive reward for increasing penetration depth, with penalties for high forces or ineffective actions). This aligns with how the LunarLander reward encourages soft landing (fuel efficiency and low impact velocity)file-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyi.
Another consideration from the RL literature is the handling of partial observability. In a real-world scenario, a burrowing robot might not directly observe the full state of the soil (e.g. the positions of every grain or the stress in the soil). In simulation, we have complete information, but we will likely restrict the state to aggregated or accessible measures (such as tip resistance force, anchor position, etc.), making it closer to what a real device could sense. This effectively can make the problem partially observable. Gadgil et al. (2020) addressed uncertainty by training and testing their agents with sensor noise and random force disturbancesfile-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyifile-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyi. The agents were still able to perform well, suggesting that the learning was robust to imperfect state information. For our project, we might not explicitly add noise at first, but it is reassuring that RL methods can handle some unobserved dynamics. If needed, techniques like recurrent neural networks or frame-stacking could be used to help the agent infer unobserved variables from past states (as is done in partially observable Markov decision processes, POMDPs).
In summary, Theme 4 relates directly to Project Aim 4, focusing on how to map the burrowing physics problem into an RL problem. The studies reviewed (especially the Lunar Lander RL implementations) confirm that a Box2D-based environment can be effectively controlled by RL algorithms, and they provide blueprints for structuring state and action spaces. A critical analysis point is ensuring the state includes enough information (e.g. current phase of the burrowing cycle, whether the anchor is engaged, the depth achieved, current soil resistance) to allow the agent to make informed decisions. Likewise, the action space should be expressive enough to cover the necessary maneuver repertoire but not so large or continuous as to make learning intractable. Based on the literature, a discrete action space handled by a deep RL agent (DQN or similar) is a practical choice for such problemsfile-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyifile-3thzfch9p1jkaewvxqwqyi. The integration of these ideas will allow the project to develop an autonomous agent that learns the optimal burrowing strategy through trial and error in simulation, fulfilling Aim 3 (designing the sequence via learning) and Aim 4 (the formal MDP setup for training).
In summary, this research aims to address these gaps. There are notable gaps that this project will address. Firstly, no prior work has combined a fast game-engine-based soil simulation with reinforcement learning for autonomous burrowing – this represents a novel integration of Themes 2 and 4. Dynamic burrowing strategies (Theme 1) have been studied via analysis and DEM, but developing an adaptive strategy through learning (as opposed to predefined sequences) is largely unexplored. Additionally, ensuring that a simplified 2D model can be tuned to honor soil physics (Theme 3) while still being used in an agent training loop raises unique challenges of calibration and stability that have not been deeply examined in RL contexts. The synthesis of these themes directly shapes the project’s methodology: we will build on the efficient simulation techniques and validated soil modeling to create an RL environment, and attempt to train an agent to discover an optimal burrowing policy. In doing so, we aim to demonstrate a proof-of-concept for intelligent, self-burrowing probes, bridging the gap between theoretical soil mechanics and practical autonomous control. 1 - Model configuration and parameters
	– Box2D
The present study utilizes the Box2D physics engine to simulate the granular medium. Box2D operates under the constraint-based contact model to model particle-level interactions (Jean, 1999; Radjai & Richefeu, 2009). In this contact model, forces are calculated by velocity-level constraints as opposed to force-penalty springs, and are solved using Coulomb’s friction law. More specifically, the constraints are integrated iteratively at each timestep, updating the new translation and rotation. Although this takes a lot of computational load per timestep, this technique allows larger timesteps compared to DEM, balancing speed and accuracy. In contrast, the discrete element method (DEM) relies on a penalty-based technique, grounded in Newton’s second law and spring-damper methods (Cundall and Stack, 1979). This method relies on force-penalty formulation, necessitating hugely reduced timesteps. While DEM is extremely useful for accurate soil behaviour representation, it lacks in the responsive model Box2D employs. Additionally, DEM does not assign the direct property to its key parameters but instead fine-tunes them to a close enough value by introducing damping. However, the main advantage of Box2D is that it adopts the friction coefficient as a direct physical input. For these reasons, Box2D was selected for this study as it is well aligned with the primary aims of this study by balancing high-volume training data generation and physical realism.

	– Validation
Prior to commencing RL training for soil-probe interactions, it becomes necessary to verify friction coefficient of the soil model is well-calibrated. A widely adopted approach involves evaluating the soil’s shear response through biaxial compression scenarios. 
Calibration relies on established theory to verify the numerical model accurately emulates granular behaviour. By aligning the simulated soil behaviour with standard theoretical frameworks, the validity of the soil model is confirmed.
	Rowe’s Theory
Rowe’s stress-dilatancy theory describes the manner in which shear and volumetric strain are related to each other during shearing of granular media – i.e. that granular soil tends to change its volume during shear deformation . By analysing the micromechanical response, the macroscopic stress-strain behaviour can be extracted. This theory expresses that the stress ratio depends on the mobilized friction angle and the dilation angle:
σ_1/σ_3 =tan^2⁡〖 (45°+ϕ_m/2)  tan^2⁡〖 (45°-ψ/2),〗 〗
where σ_1 is the major principal stress, σ_3 is the minor principal stress, ϕ_m is the mobilised friction angle, and ψ is the dilation angle.
During the early loading stages, loose sand has been observed to show a contractive response during shear, often characterised by a negative dilatancy (ψ < 0) – meaning volume reduced - and low shear strength. Conversely, dense sand has been observed to manifest positive dilatancy (ψ > 0), increasing resistance to shear due to net volume increase. This underscores the critical role of Rowe’s theory in differentiating between loose sand and dense sand, allowing for either to be directly identified and validated by monitoring its shear response behaviour. 
For instance, Pytos et al. (2015) implemented Rowe’s theoretical framework to calibrate his granular soil in biaxial simulations, using the Box2D physics engine. The results showed close agreement between the simulation data and  the Rowe’s curve, shown in the Figure below.

3.3 - Particle and Contact Parameters
Particle and contact parameters were calibrated to replicate the mechanical behavior of dry sand. Each particle was assigned a density of (\rho = 2650 , \text{kg/m}^3), scaled for 2D simulations to achieve a bulk density of approximately 1600 kg/m³, accounting for the void ratio. The interparticle friction coefficient was set to (\mu = 0.5), corresponding to an internal friction angle of (\phi \approx 26^\circ), consistent with medium-dense sands (Arroyo et al., 2011). Damping was applied with a coefficient (\beta = 0.1) to stabilize dynamics by dissipating energy during collisions.

The soil sample was initialized by generating polydisperse dodecagonal particles within the chamber. Particles were distributed according to a gradation curve with (D_{50} = 0.04–0.05 , \text{m}). To achieve uniform density, particles were generated in layers from the chamber bottom upward. Each layer settled under gravity with a damping coefficient (\beta = 0.1) to dissipate kinetic energy, preventing excessive bouncing and ensuring static equilibrium. This layered approach minimized disturbances and produced a consistent density profile.
The system was equilibrated until the maximum particle velocity fell below (v_{\text{threshold}} = 0.01 , \text{m/s}), indicating a stable configuration. The final void ratio was verified to match medium-dense sand ((\approx 0.6)), ensuring a representative initial state for probe penetration simulations.
Learning-Compatible Simulation Design
The simulation sequence involved four actions, modeled after the stages of burrowing employed by the razor clam. Generally the razor clam burrows by: anchor expansion, tip extension, simultaneous anchor contraction/tip expansion, anchor retraction. To enable RL, these motions were discretised into a simplified action space within an MDP framework. The MDP framework is used in RL to characterise the training cycle of RL in the following array {s, a, r, s’}. Now I will define each subset of the array for this simulation.
To enable RL, the probe’s motion was simplified into a discrete action space within an MDP framework. The choice for a discrete instead of continuous… The burrowing sequence comprises:
	Shaft expansion ((+v_x)) until high anchorage force.
	Tip extension ((+v_y)) to a limit.
	Shaft contraction ((-v_x)) to original size.
	Downward burrowing by the tip extension distance.
The action space is (a \in {(0,0), (+v_x,0), (-v_x,0), (0,+v_y), (0,-v_y)}), with states including probe position, forces, and soil properties. Rewards may prioritize penetration depth or energy efficiency. This “gamified” approach, inspired by RL benchmarks, simplifies control while enabling systematic exploration of bio-inspired burrowing strategies.
